## Overview

Most AI tools are a clever assistant in a box. You ask, copy the answer somewhere else, and start over next time. I wanted something different: agents that hold context for weeks, do small jobs on a schedule, and know exactly what they’re allowed to do without asking me.

StraatClaw is the result. It’s a small team of named agents I designed and run every day on top of Hermes Agent. I didn’t build Hermes. It’s Nous Research’s open-source framework, and I use it as the plumbing. What I designed is everything people actually experience: who does what, what each agent may do alone, when it has to stop and ask, and how that ask shows up on my phone.

The core question turned out to be a product design question, not a model question: when will someone let an agent act for them?

## Challenge

Four rules I would not give up:

- **Predictable.** I should know what each agent will and won’t do before it acts.
- **Interruptible.** Anything with consequences outside my own notes should wait for me.
- **Easy to approve.** The ask should come to me where I already am, and be quick to answer.
- **Inspectable.** I should be able to read what an agent “knows” and fix it.

## Why it mattered

There was no formal research study. This section is why the design exists.

When one agent can read everything, write anywhere, and act on my behalf, I can’t predict it — so I can’t trust it. Teams hit the same wall when they bring agents into real work: a traveler handing off a trip, a team letting an agent touch shared tickets, a developer letting an agent change infrastructure.

The brief I set: predictable, interruptible, easy to approve, inspectable.

## Solution

Instead of one giant agent, I designed a small organization. Every agent gets a short profile: what it does, what it’s not responsible for, what it reads, what it writes, and when it hands off to me.

- StraatBot, the orchestrator and chief-of-staff layer. It captures, triages, routes work, tracks open loops, and escalates to me.
- Scout, research. Morning briefs, signals, watchlists, and a weekly synthesis.
- Finley, finance. Cash flow, budget tracking, and the numbers behind decisions. Finley works from a private data inbox only it reads; other agents get a status or a flag, never raw figures.
- Helpers by role for household operations, food and groceries, and travel planning.
- A product design squad that runs a staged flow: PRD, lo-fi wireframes, mid-fi specs, a design kit, then a runnable prototype, based on my public Cursor rules.

Hermes exposes capabilities; permissions are granted to named agents that run on Hermes.

## Design Decisions

The most useful thing I wrote wasn’t a prompt. It was a permission matrix: six levels that spell out what an agent can do alone and what it has to bring back.

<PermissionMatrix />

Every agent has a default level. Scout can write reports but never contacts anyone. StraatBot can open a bug issue and hand off a fix, but it can’t publish. One rule sits above all of it: human judgment gates external action.

Approvals arrive in chat, not in a dashboard. Telegram is for quick captures, status, approvals, and the morning brief. Slack is for real work, one thread per task.

The approval prompt (Allow Once / Allow Session / Always Allow / Deny) comes from Hermes Agent’s built-in command approval. The design work is the levels and rules that decide when it fires and where it reaches me.

## Reflection

I’m not trying to remove anyone’s judgment, including my own. I’m trying to give it better context and a little more leverage. The idea fits in one line: I don’t want one giant agent. I want a small organization of bounded agents.

What I learned about trust:

- Trust comes from limits, not intelligence.
- The approval moment is the product.
- Silence has to mean no.
- Honest output builds more trust than impressive output.
- Memory needs an owner and a home.
